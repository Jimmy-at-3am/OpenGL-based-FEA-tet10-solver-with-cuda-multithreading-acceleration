# PrintProof — Conjugate Gradient as energy minimization

I built PrintProof, a free FEA engine designed around the 3D-printed parts students actually make. To predict how a part bends, it breaks the part into a fine mesh of hundreds of thousands of points, each free to shift a little in any direction. The shape the part finally takes is just one arrangement of all those points out of countless possible ones. The usual way to find it is to solve that whole coupled system of equations directly, but that generates so much intermediate calculation that memory runs out fast. So instead of solving the equations directly, I used a principle from physics called minimum potential energy. A loaded structure comes to rest in the shape that stores the least energy — the way a ball rolls to the bottom of a valley. Finding the part's shape becomes finding the lowest point of an energy landscape, and that landscape has just one true bottom: the shape the part actually takes. Getting there efficiently is its own problem. Plain downhill steps waste motion, zig-zagging and undoing their own progress. To avoid that, I turned to a method called Conjugate Gradient, which picks each step so it never spoils the ground the earlier ones already covered — and never has to take the full system apart. A model with half a million unknowns then fits in a few hundred megabytes of memory, where solving it directly would demand many gigabytes.
